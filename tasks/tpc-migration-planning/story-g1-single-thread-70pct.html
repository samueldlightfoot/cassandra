TPC migration · Phase 2 · gate G1 · story edition

The thread that had to feed a PM9A3 alone

Why the benchmark pins ONE thread when TPC runs one per CPU, and why the bar is 70% rather than 100% — traced through this evening's live numbers from the rig. Skimming? The setup and the ⚡ boxes carry the spine. The general rule comes last.

The setup

  • Rig: 12-core Hetzner box, Samsung PM9A3 960 GB datacenter NVMe, kernel 6.8, governor pinned to performance, irqbalance stopped.
  • Shape: 4 KiB random reads, O_DIRECT (bypasses the page cache), cache-cold, 32 GiB file — the sstable cache-miss shape.
  • QD (queue depth) — how many I/O ops are in flight at once. A synchronous pread64 thread is always QD1: it sleeps until each read returns.
  • Contender A: one thread pinned to core 2 + an io_uring ring, allowed up to QD64.
  • Contender B: 50 psync (pread64) threads floating over all 12 cores — today's Cassandra read-stage, idealized (fio does no locking, no parsing, no GC).

The governing law: under TPC a shard gets exactly one core, and whatever I/O the shard needs must be raised from that one core.

Chapter 1 · One core, sixty-four slots

covers: why the bench pins a single thread when TPC is one-thread-per-CPU

TPC on this box means 12 shards, each one thread on its own core, each owning a slice of the token range, each with its own ring. Shards share nothing — so 12 shards perform like 12 × (one shard), until they collide at the device. The unit to measure is therefore one shard: pin one thread to core 2 and ask what it can raise. Running 12 rings at once would only measure the device's ceiling — a spec-sheet number — and tell us nothing about whether a lone shard thread can cover its own cache misses.

At QD1 the thread reads like pread64 does: ask, sleep, repeat. Each round trip costs ~65 µs, so it raises 15,297 IOPS — almost identical to a single psync thread (15,389). The ring's escape hatch is depth: prepare 64 reads, submit them with one syscall, reap completions as they land.

core 2, one thread, 120 s of 4 KiB cold reads QD1 : ask→sleep 65 µs→ask = 15,297 IOPS QD64: 64 in flight, 1 enter = ?
Checkpoint 1 — QD1 gave 15.3k. What does the same thread raise at QD64? Hint: 64 × 15.3k would be ~980k. But what else must this one core pay for, per op?

260,103 IOPS — and the wall isn't the device, it's the core. The cell ran at 100% CPU (18% user, 82% kernel): ~3.8 µs of CPU per op for submission, ext4/block-layer work, and completion handling. QD32 scored 258,099 — flat vs QD64 — the signature of CPU saturation, not device saturation.

Depth stops helping the moment the submitting core, not the drive, becomes the bottleneck.

The single-thread cell is not a handicap, it is the experiment. One shard's I/O engine is the unit TPC scales by — measure the unit, and N shards follow by symmetry. 12 rings at once would re-measure the PM9A3's spec sheet; one pinned ring measures the architecture.

Chapter 2 · Fifty threads, twelve cores, one bar

covers: why the pass bar is 70% of the psync-50 baseline, not 100%

The baseline arm is deliberately unfair: 50 threads spread across all 12 cores, presenting ~QD50 to the device the way today's read stage does — by thread count. It raised 417,566 IOPS. So the matchup the gate scores is one core versus the whole box:

The gate asks: how far below today's whole-box ceiling may a single shard's ceiling fall?

First, where there is no problem: box-wide, submission is over-provisioned either way. Twelve shards × 260k = 3.1M IOPS of submission capability against a device that tops out near ~500k — the PM9A3 stays the limit, exactly as it is today. G1 exists for the one scenario that arithmetic doesn't cover: a hot partition lives on exactly one shard, and that shard's ring cannot be helped by the other eleven. Today a hot partition's cache misses spread across the 50-thread pool and can draw the device's full 417,566; under pure TPC the same partition tops out at what one core can raise — 260,103.

Checkpoint 2 — over 120 s, how many context switches did each arm pay? Hint: a sleeping pread64 thread must be woken for every completion. The ring polls a mmap'd completion queue.

psync-50: 50,070,387 switches — exactly one per op. The ring: 1,744 total. Four orders of magnitude, measured by pidstat on the same box within the hour.

In real Cassandra that 50M/op cost class travels with friends fio can't simulate — scheduler wake-ups landing on hot caches, lock handoffs, stage-to-stage hops. Those are the costs TPC exists to delete, and this bench structurally cannot credit them.

Now the 70% fits in one sentence: it caps how far the hot shard may fall. Today that partition can draw 417,566 IOPS. The gate tolerates its ceiling dropping to 70% of that — 292,296 IOPS. One core raised 260,103. Miss the line, and the PoC must add a helper pool: a few I/O threads that any hot shard hands its cache-miss reads to.

Why tolerate any drop at all? Two buy-backs. The ring deletes the fifty-million-switch bill you just saw, plus the friends fio can't simulate. And load that lands entirely on one shard is the worst point of the distribution, not the normal one. Why exactly 70, and not 65 or 80? Judgment, not math. Its force is that it was written down before the sweep ran — so today's 62% cannot argue its way to a pass.

One question left. The core ran flat-out at 100% — the thread could not have asked the device for more. Why hold that against the ring? Because the bench hands the ring its best possible deal: a whole core with nothing to do but I/O. A real shard thread lives differently. It parses, merges, and serves with that same core.

Checkpoint 3 — a realistic shard spends 80% of its core on query work, leaving 20% for the ring. What read ceiling does that leave? Hint: 260,103 IOPS consumed 100% of the core — the cost is ~3.8 µs of CPU per op, and it scales.

~52,000 IOPS — a fifth of the bench number. The cost is per-op CPU, so the ceiling shrinks with whatever core fraction the shard can spare: 0.2 × 260,103 ≈ 52k.

That is why CPU-bound matters: I/O submission and query execution compete for the same core, and the helper pool is how a hot shard escapes the squeeze. It is also the recoverable failure mode — 82% of the per-op cost is kernel work (page pinning, fd lookup) that registered buffers and files attack; the annex cells test exactly that. Device-bound would have been final.

The baseline gets twelve cores and pays fifty million context switches; the ring gets one core and pays none. 70% bounds how far a hot shard may fall below today's whole-box ceiling before the helper pool stops being optional — fail the gate and TPC proceeds anyway, with one design decision made for us by measurement. And the gate can't be gamed by weak baselines: psync-50 here is a best case — fio does no Cassandra work at all.
